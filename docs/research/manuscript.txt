Abstract—Multi-tenant containers require both enforceable
boundaries and predictable resource access. IsoLab integrates
thirty conﬁguration checks, resource controls, event rules and
startup-integrity enforcement with a reproducible interference
evaluation. A new single-host pilot comprises 160 runs of a
payments service and Spark RandomForest and SVM work-
loads. Under sustained contention, the hardened resource-control
adaptation reduces median victim latency or job time by 61.0,
35.1 and 40.1 percent, respectively, while reducing competing-
workload throughput by approximately 64–66 percent. Us-
ing common telemetry and execution-level held-out splits, a
lightweight resource-logistic detector achieves F1 1.000 versus
0.941 for a 2024-inspired autoencoder/LSTM adaptation on
payments; both reach 1.000 on RandomForest. On SVM, logistic
F1 is 0.400 versus 0.857 for the neural adaptation. Logistic ﬁtting
takes milliseconds rather than tens of seconds, but accuracy
depends on workload and the small test sets do not establish
general superiority. Historical security experiments additionally
validate twelve blocked exposure paths, independent conﬁgura-
tion faults and startup-integrity decisions. The contribution is a
reproducible evaluation of isolation beneﬁts, resource cost and
detector tradeoffs, with explicit separation between published
reference numbers and locally measured adaptations.
Index Terms—cloud security, site reliability engineering, multi-
tenancy, container isolation, noisy neighbor
I. I NTRODUCTION
Cloud tenants depend on boundaries that preserve conﬁden-
tiality and predictable resource access. A workload can suffer
latency inﬂation when another tenant exhausts shared proces-
sor capacity, even without a direct compromise. Conversely,
healthy request latency does not establish that networks,
storage, or management interfaces are isolated. Assessing a
cloud security mechanism therefore requires complementary
evidence about resource interference, boundary enforcement,
and detection.
Two recent studies motivate this work. Horchulhack et
al. [1] investigate provider-side detection of container in-
terference using resource observations and learned temporal
classiﬁcation. Jarkas et al. [2] organize container exploits and
defenses into a security taxonomy. These studies motivate a
practical question: how can selected defensive recommenda-
tions be connected to reproducible enforcement and reliability
evidence in an accessible laboratory? IsoLab answers with
a scoped implementation and explicit evidence boundaries.
Its original R1–R4 requirement labels and ﬁve-axis check
grouping retain their historical provenance in Gonzalez et
al. [3]; they are not attributed to the recent papers.
IsoLab connects security controls to site reliability engineer-
ing (SRE) through a measurable latency objective and tenant-
level resource observations. The study addresses ﬁve research
questions:
• RQ1: Does the hardened conﬁguration reduce processor
interference relative to an intentionally insecure baseline?
• RQ2: Are the tested tenant boundaries closed while
required application and monitoring connectivity remains
functional?
• RQ3: Are benign isolation-breaking triggers detected
within speciﬁed latency limits and with the expected
workload attribution?
• RQ4: Do selected defense checks and startup integrity
enforcement reject their speciﬁed conﬁguration and ﬁle-
integrity faults?
• RQ5: How do a lightweight resource classiﬁer, a 2024-
inspired temporal model and existing rules trade detection
quality against ﬁtting cost on common measurements?
Our contributions are: (1) a 160-run matched pilot compar-
ing interference protection, competing throughput and detector
accuracy/cost on three workloads; (2) a machine-readable
crosswalk connecting selected 2025 survey defense themes
to existing conﬁguration checks and complementary observa-
tions; (3) startup hashing and executable/entry-script allowlist-
ing, implementing a selected aspect of mitigation M2; (4) inde-
pendent conﬁguration-fault and integrity challenges, alongside
historical latency, segmentation, and detection evidence; and
(5) fail-closed evidence predicates and an interactive research
interface. The system integrates established controls rather
than introducing a new isolation primitive. Historical live runs
and newly executed local challenges are reported separately.

B. Container Threats and Empirical Security Evaluation
Sultan et al. [4] distinguish security relationships within
containers, between containers, and between containers and
the host. This motivates making the trusted provider and
host boundary explicit. Lin et al. [5] evaluate Linux-container
protection using real exploit cases and examine interacting
kernel defenses. Their exploit-based evidence addresses a
stronger attack mechanism than our benign probes and con-
ﬁguration mutations. IsoLab’s closed endpoints and startup
decisions consequently cannot be interpreted as equivalent
exploit-resistance evidence.
C. Virtualization Security Requirements
The source survey [ 3] treats virtualization as an architec-
tural security concern and emphasizes isolation and cross-VM
attacks. IsoLab implements the engineering implications of its
future-work requirements. It does not implement every branch
of the taxonomy or claim that the historical literature deﬁcit
persists. Its check-coverage percentages quantify conﬁguration
posture and cannot be compared numerically with the survey’s
citation shares.
D. Containers and Resource Observation
NIST SP 800-190 describes containers as operating-system
virtualization and discusses their security risks [ 6]. Containers
share a kernel, whereas hardware virtual machines use a
different isolation boundary. This work is consequently a
container adaptation of VM-oriented requirements, rather than
a reproduction of a KVM, Xen, or VMware deployment.
Felter et al. [7] compare virtual-machine and Linux-
container performance across workloads. This motivates dis-
tinguishing virtualization overhead from the interference ques-
tion examined here. IsoLab compares two container conﬁgu-
rations and does not measure a container-versus-VM perfor-
mance advantage.
Linux cgroup v2 provides hierarchical resource control [ 8],
while Pressure Stall Information (PSI) measures time lost to
resource pressure [ 9]. IsoLab combines these interfaces with
conﬁguration snapshots and engine events. Its explicit service-
level objective (SLO) follows the SRE practice of assessing
reliability through measurable service indicators [ 10]. Here,
the SLO is an experimental latency criterion at a ﬁxed offered
load, not a contractual availability guarantee.
Dean and Barroso [11] explain why high-percentile latency
matters in large services. IsoLab uses p99 as a tenant-visible
indicator, while recognizing that its synthetic single-service
workload does not reproduce the fan-out behavior or scale
considered in that work.
E. Isolation and Scheduling Defenses
V aradarajanet al. [12] investigate scheduler-based defenses
against cross-VM side channels, including minimum run-
time guarantees and per-core state cleansing in Xen. That
work evaluates a speciﬁc attack mechanism. IsoLab instead
measures tenant-visible latency, conﬁguration exposure, and
benign event detection. Disjoint reported CPU allocations in
this study must not be interpreted as demonstrating side-
channel resistance. No comparative benchmark against that
scheduler or commercial security products was performed; the
later pilot compares four detection approaches on common
telemetry.
III. S YSTEM DESIGN
A. Scope and Threat Model
The protected assets are tenant application responsive-
ness, tenant storage markers, process visibility, and access to
provider management and telemetry interfaces. An untrusted
tenant may consume its permitted CPU resources and attempt
connections or reads from its own execution context. The
testbed also exercises potentially unsafe management opera-
tions through controlled operator triggers, such as starting a
privileged workload or attaching a tenant to a foreign network.
These triggers validate observation; they do not assume that
an isolated tenant can invoke the provider’s management API.
The provider plane, conﬁguration policy, host kernel, en-
gine, and socket proxy form the trusted computing base.
Workload identity labels are administrative metadata, not cryp-
tographic attestation. Kernel compromise, malicious provider
administration, hardware side channels, secret recovery, and
arbitrary exploit resistance are outside the evaluated threat

B. Architecture and Conﬁguration Proﬁles
The tenant plane contains tenant A, a CPU-sensitive pay-
ments service; tenant B, a batch workload and processor stress
job; and tenant C, a legacy workload used as a probe origin.
The provider plane contains the auditor, a restricted engine-
socket proxy, metadata canary, Prometheus, and Grafana.
Fig. 1 summarizes the observation and reporting paths. The
provider conﬁguration is held constant across the insecure and
hardened tenant proﬁles.
In the hardened proﬁle, workloads run as user 10001, drop
capabilities, enable no-new-privileges, and use read-only root
ﬁlesystems. Process and memory limits constrain resource
use. Separate internal networks and volumes establish tenant
boundaries. A topology planner allocates disjoint reported
physical-core groups to tenants and separate groups to the
provider and load generator; CPU quotas further constrain exe-
cution. Nested virtualization means that reported core topology
does not establish exclusive use of physical host cores or
caches.
Docker exposes CPU quotas, CPU afﬁnity, and memory
constraints as distinct resource controls [ 13]. Seccomp restricts
system-call access [ 14]; its conﬁgured presence reduces ex-
posed functionality but does not prove the absence of kernel
defects. Several controls change together, so the experiment
estimates the effect of the conﬁguration bundle.

D. Runtime Observation and Detection
Nine rules observe tenant execution, privileged starts,
engine-socket mounts, host-namespace joins, missing identity,
cross-tenant network attachment, canary access, excessive
shared-CPU use, and out-of-memory events. Docker’s event
interface exposes container lifecycle and execution events [ 16];
IsoLab adds project-speciﬁc classiﬁcation and attribution. Re-
source counters and PSI are sampled every ﬁve seconds. The
noisy-neighbor rule identiﬁes sustained excessive processor
use on CPUs shared with another active tenant. Detection
records include rule, workload, expected attribution evidence,
and timestamps.
Rules describe observable behaviors rather than inferred
malicious intent. Legitimate maintenance can generate similar
events. Detection state is bounded and held in memory, so
durable incident retention requires additional infrastructure.

The experiments exercise eight trigger types; the out-of-
memory rule is not included in the live detection evaluation.
E. Selected ACM Defenses and Startup Integrity
The crosswalk records six selected themes: host-mount
restriction, root-ﬁlesystem immutability, least privilege, private
process namespaces, syscall ﬁltering, and resource allocation.
Mount restriction implements the host-volume aspect of M1;
immutability implements M3. Other associations use the sur-
vey’s attack surfaces, including E18/E32, A6.1/E23, A7, and
E34. These are our defensive engineering associations, not
claims that a conﬁguration check reproduces a CVE or extends
the survey’s own mitigation table. A theme is conﬁguration-
veriﬁed only when every mapped check and target passes
without a waiver. Missing, duplicate, skipped, erroneous, or
waived evidence cannot establish veriﬁcation. No percentage
of the complete exploit taxonomy is inferred.
For a selected aspect of M2, image builds produce SHA-256
allowlists of the interpreter, startup application, probe script,
and integrity launcher; the batch image additionally includes
the stress executable. SHA-256 is speciﬁed in the Secure
Hash Standard [ 17]; using that hash does not authenticate
the manifest or establish trusted build provenance. Hardened
workloads enable a launcher that validates all recorded hashes
and permits only an allowlisted executable. For Python, it ad-
ditionally requires an allowlisted entry script, rejecting inline
or module-mode execution through this launcher. A mismatch,
missing ﬁle, unlisted command, or invalid manifest returns exit
code 78 before application execution. The ordinary hardened
batch job also uses this launcher. The insecure proﬁle leaves
enforcement disabled as a comparison control.
The build environment, manifest, launcher, and engine re-
main trusted. An engine administrator can replace the entry-
point, and an arbitrary process started through another path
is not mediated. This mechanism is startup integrity checking
rather than continuous attestation, complete application con-
trol, or protection against a compromised image builder. It
complements the read-only ﬁlesystem instead of replacing it.
IV. E XPERIMENTAL METHOD
A. Environment and Evidence Provenance
The historical E1–E3 measurements were generated on Oc-
tober 12, 2026, under source identiﬁer dffa2c70c553. The
environment records six reported physical cores, twelve logi-
cal CPUs, Docker 29.2.1, kernel 6.6.87.2-microsoft-standard-
WSL2, and cgroup v2. The artifact contains protocols, per-run
summaries, outcomes, and machine-readable hypotheses [ 18].
The revised defense crosswalk and E4–E5 challenges were
executed on November 11, 2026. They do not update or relabel
the historical latency measurements; those predate the new
startup integrity mechanism.
B. E1 Processor Interference
A constant-arrival-rate k6 workload sends 50 requests per
second to tenant A ’s/work endpoint. This open-model execu-
tor schedules iterations independently of response time while
sufﬁcient virtual users are available [ 19]; dropped iterations are
therefore an explicit validity condition. Each arm has 15 sec-
onds of discarded warm-up and 90 measured seconds. Three
repetitions per proﬁle and arm produce twelve measured runs.
Tenant B executes a CPU-only stress-ng job. A separate
load-generator allocation limits measurement interference. The
application performs 2500 SHA-256 rounds per request and
reports server work time separately from request latency.
The four arms are insecure baseline, insecure noisy, hard-
ened baseline, and hardened noisy. Arms are interleaved with
cooldowns. Runs are invalid if arrival iterations are dropped,
failures exceed 1%, or the noisy job fails the protocol’s
processor-activity ﬂoor. The committed full run satisﬁes these
validity conditions. The experimental SLO is a 99th-percentile
request latency of at most 25 ms.
For proﬁle p, the interference factor is
IFp = medianr q.99,p,noisy,rmedianr q.99,p,baseline,r , (2)
where q.99,p,a,r is the request-latency quantile for repetition r
in arm a. This is a ratio of medians of run quantiles, not a
quantile of pooled requests. Three run repetitions, rather than
the number of requests, determine the experimental replication
count.
C. E2 Boundary Probes
Twelve read-only or connection-based probes test cross-
tenant DNS and IP access, a published host route, metadata
and telemetry access, internet egress, process visibility, engine-
socket access, host-ﬁle visibility, another tenant’s nonsecret
marker ﬁle, effective capabilities, and tenant-B-to-A connec-
tivity. Three positive controls verify application health access,
provider monitoring access, and local loopback connectivity.
Each probe returns allowed, blocked, or error. A passing hard-
ened result requires all twelve boundaries explicitly blocked
and all three controls allowed; an error is not evidence of
isolation.
D. E3 Detection V alidation
Each proﬁle ﬁrst runs under normal load for 120 seconds,
followed by benign triggers corresponding to unsafe execution,
privilege, mounts, namespaces, missing labels, network at-
tachment, canary access, and CPU contention. Ordinary event
detections have a ﬁve-second limit, while the sustained noisy-
neighbor rule has a ninety-second limit. Attribution must agree
with the protocol’s expected identity, which can be unknown
for unlabelled workloads. Hardened trigger T7 is expected to
remain silent because its dedicated CPU allocation prevents
the shared-CPU detection condition. Event and observation
timestamps use the same VM clock domain.
E. Hypotheses and Analysis Plan
The protocol speciﬁes H1: insecure IF ≥ 2; H2: hardened
IF ≤ 1.25 while meeting the latency target; optional H3:
increased server time under SMT sharing; H4: noisy-neighbor
detection in the insecure proﬁle and silence in the hardened
proﬁle; H5: at least ten insecure paths open; H6: zero hardened

paths open with passing controls; and H7: timely detection,
expected attribution, and no observed false positives. The
protocol logs detector calibration changes after smoke tests.
A later evidence-validation correction strengthens acceptance
predicates without changing thresholds or historical measure-
ments.
We report run-level medians and ranges rather than statisti-
cal signiﬁcance from three repetitions. For detection, illustra-
tive Wilson intervals [20] show the uncertainty associated with
small success counts; heterogeneous deterministic triggers do
not justify treating those intervals as representative attack-
detection estimates. A separately qualiﬁed Poisson calculation
illustrates the limited information supplied by a short quiet
window.
F . E4 Conﬁguration Faults and E5 Startup Integrity
E4 starts with the committed hardened snapshot and in-
dependently mutates tenant A ten times: privileged execution,
retained capabilities, unconﬁned seccomp, root execution, host
PID sharing, missing CPU quota, writable root ﬁlesystem,
sensitive host bind, engine-socket mount, and absent CPU
placement. Each mutation starts from a fresh deep copy.
Success requires the speciﬁed tenant/check pair to pass before
mutation and explicitly fail afterward. Existing unrelated host
failures cannot satisfy this criterion. This is a saved-snapshot
checker-sensitivity experiment, not ten live attacks or ten
independent deployments.
For mutation j, let b j and m j denote the targeted baseline
and mutated check outcomes. The acceptance indicator is
d j = 1{b j = pass ∧ m j = fail}. (3)
Only the same tenant/check pair is compared. Missing or
ambiguous targets earn zero credit, and every case must satisfy
the indicator for the study gate to pass. This distinguishes
detection of an introduced regression from rediscovery of an
existing failure.
E5 executes the actual launcher in isolated subprocesses
with a temporary application that writes an execution marker.
The clean case must exit successfully and create the marker;
modiﬁed, missing, or unlisted scripts and an invalid manifest
must return 78 without creating it. These ﬁve deliberately
selected cases validate startup decisions, not general attack-
detection accuracy. A separate container check exercises clean
and bind-mounted altered application ﬁles in each of the three
built tenant images. Neither challenge modiﬁes a deployed
tenant or a published historical result.

D. Selected Defense V alidation Results
E4 detected all ten speciﬁed conﬁguration faults, with a
passing baseline and explicit failing tenant target in each
case. Four of six mapped themes are conﬁguration-veriﬁed
for the historical hardened report; none is veriﬁed for the
insecure report. The two remaining hardened themes con-
tain provider exception targets and consequently receive no
complete-veriﬁcation credit under the strict all-target rule.
E5 passed all ﬁve startup challenges: the clean application
executed, while four invalid cases were rejected before execu-
tion. Six additional checks in real container images accepted
each clean application and rejected its altered-ﬁle counterpart.
These outcomes answer RQ4 for the selected inputs. They do
not estimate sensitivity to unseen attacks, and conﬁguration
veriﬁcation does not establish resistance to every exploit
sharing the survey category.
The added contribution is the connection among a de-
fense recommendation, its enforcement mechanism, and a
reproducible counterexample test, combined with separate
reliability evidence. The separate pilot in Section VIII-B
compares a locally adapted neural architecture with alternative
detectors. Its workload-dependent ﬁndings do not establish
general superiority over the source paper or broader security.

seven expected triggers with no alerts in a thirty-second quiet
window, and passed the conﬁguration gate with the two pre-
existing host waivers. This shortened smoke check does not
replace the historical full-length evaluation or establish the
latency performance of the revised system.
VI. D ISCUSSION AND REPRODUCIBILITY
A. Security and Reliability Interpretation
The three experiments provide complementary evidence.
E1 measures the reliability effect of CPU contention; E2
checks concrete exposure paths; E3 evaluates observation and
attribution of selected behaviors. None can replace the others.

In particular, satisfying a latency target cannot establish con-
ﬁdentiality, and detecting a management event cannot prove
that the corresponding action was prevented. The combined
results support the feasibility of integrating posture controls
and SRE observations within the stated trust boundary.
Dedicated allocations also constrain resource sharing. The
new pilot measures competing-workload throughput but not
economic consolidation cost; its latency improvements must be
read alongside the observed throughput reduction. A deploy-
ment decision requires capacity and utilization measurements
alongside these isolation indicators.
B. Evidence V alidation
The ﬁnal evidence predicates require the complete unique
E2 probe set with explicit outcomes and the complete unique
E3 trigger set with timely detection, expected attribution,
required silence, and no false-positive records. This prevents
an erroneous or missing probe from being counted as a closed
path and prevents summary detection counts from hiding
attribution failures. Publisher verdicts and live gates share
these predicates. The stricter predicates pass the historical
results.
Thirteen evidence-gate regression cases exercise valid and
corrupted inputs, including missing, duplicate, late, erroneous,
open, and misattributed outcomes. Additional research tests
verify mutation independence, baseline contamination rejec-
tion, fail-closed mapping, references, provenance hashes, and
startup challenge outcomes. Previously reported software val-
idation recorded 182 Python tests passed and one skipped; the
skipped case depends on an unpublished literature extension.
That revision also recorded 52 frontend tests passing, together
with lint and production-build checks. The pilot adds ﬁve
comparison-utility tests, a model forward/backward smoke
check, and independent recalculation of all 160 run sum-
maries and twelve detector/workload results. Software checks
establish internal consistency rather than a new performance
benchmark.
C. Artifact Availability
The source and machine-readable measurements are avail-
able in the project repository [ 18]. The experiment protocol
records conﬁgurations, metrics, thresholds, and deviations.
Full reproduction uses the lab orchestrator to check the host,
execute both proﬁles with the full experiment variant, and
publish the generated results. The reanalysis script indepen-
dently recalculates scores, latency summaries, run validity, and
detection outcomes, and records SHA-256 hashes of its inputs.
The manuscript and IEEEtran source are supplied with the
artifact.
An additional literature-replication pipeline is not a com-
pleted result of this paper. Its blind human annotation remains
unﬁnished; development-set classiﬁer metrics and unvalidated
literature-trend hypotheses are excluded. No inference about
the persistence of the 2012 literature deﬁcit is drawn from that
extension.
VII. T HREATS TO VALIDITY
A. Internal and Construct V alidity
The insecure baseline is deliberately weak and is not a
measured average production conﬁguration. Several controls
change together, preventing causal attribution to individual
settings. Monitoring overhead is unquantiﬁed because no
monitoring-disabled arm was measured. Check coverage de-
pends on project-selected severities and applicability rules; it
is not a validated risk score. The detector observes behaviors
that can also occur during legitimate maintenance.
The metadata canary measures access to a decoy rather
than secret recovery or prevention of arbitrary exﬁltration.
No kernel escape, speculative-execution exploit, or cache side
channel was attempted. Administrative identity labels do not
supply cryptographic identity. The provider plane is trusted,
and some rules observe operator-generated events that tenants
cannot necessarily generate from within their conﬁnement.
B. External and Statistical V alidity
Historical E1 uses one machine, a synthetic service, ﬁxed
offered load and three repetitions per arm. The new pilot
extends to three workloads with ten runs per cell, but retains
one host and short observation windows. It excludes storage
and network contention, multiple hosts, scaling, sustained
incidents, and workload churn. The trigger set is deterministic
and too small to establish general detection sensitivity or a
representative false-alarm rate. The pilot implements baselines
locally; it does not benchmark the source authors’ released
executable or a production security tool.
Docker Desktop’s nested virtualization limits physical-core
interpretation and visibility into provider mitigations. Two
host ﬁndings remain waived, and mandatory access control is
not evaluated on this host. The measurement source identiﬁer
predates later repository changes; ﬁxture and schema checks
do not prove that the ﬁnal revision reproduces identical live
measurements. Historical E1 lacks request-level traces in its
published package. The new pilot retains request records,
Spark job logs, telemetry, execution splits, predictions and
model artifacts; its source hashes and manifest identify the
local experimental revision.

A. Positioning Against Resource-Management Systems
Performance isolation is already an established research
problem. PARTIES [ 21] studies QoS-aware resource parti-
tioning for colocated interactive services. Caladan [ 22] uses
fast scheduling and contention signals, including memory-
hierarchy and hyperthread interference. These studies moti-
vate measuring background throughput and bursty workloads
alongside victim latency: static separation may protect one
workload at a utilization cost. IsoLab does not implement their
schedulers or benchmark against their published performance
numbers. Its distinction is the joint, auditable evidence for con-
ﬁguration enforcement, tenant boundaries, integrity checks and
interference. That engineering integration does not establish a
new scheduling algorithm.
B. Completed Single-Host Pilot
On November 17, 2026, we completed 160 independently
executed runs on Docker Desktop 29.2.1, WSL2 kernel
6.6.87.2, twelve logical CPUs and approximately 7.43 GiB
of Docker-visible memory. The seeded, randomized schedule
used ten runs per cell: two conﬁgurations and four interference
scenarios for payments (80 runs), and two conﬁgurations and
two scenarios for each Spark workload (80 runs). Collection,
ﬁtting and reporting ﬁnished in 4 h 29 min. Smoke runs used
to check execution were excluded.
The shared conﬁguration placed the victim on logical CPUs
4–5 and an eight-process competing workload on CPUs 4–11.
The hardened adaptation placed the competitor on CPUs 6–
11, applied a two-CPU quota per tenant, dropped capabilities
and enabled no-new-privileges. Observer and load-generation
work used CPUs 0–3. This performance-control adaptation
is distinct from the complete security conﬁguration used in
E1–E5; it does not repeat their boundary or integrity tests.
Both proﬁles used the same application and competing work.
Resource allocation and quota effects remain bundled in this
pilot.

C. Common Telemetry and Model Adaptation
The collector retained sixteen engine/process aggregate fea-
tures for each of the victim and competing containers: CPU
utilization, memory, process count, network and I/O rates,
throttling, CPU pressure, process CPU and resident memory,
threads, process I/O, and context switches. It required a trusted
host-PID observer, SYS_PTRACE, the Docker API, and read-
only cgroup access. Application latency and job time were
excluded from detector inputs. Missing process access failed
collection; disappearing processes were skipped.
The neural baseline adapts the architecture documented
in the 2024 study [ 1]: dense encoder widths 512/256/128,
a 20-dimensional sigmoid bottleneck and mirrored decoder,
followed by LSTM layers of 1024/512 units, dense layers
of 512/256 units and two-class softmax. Our input has 32
aggregate features from two containers, rather than the original
411-feature distributed service representation. We use train-
only min/max scaling clipped to [0, 1] for the sigmoid decoder,
a six-sample temporal window, and current runtime libraries.
The topology, feature pipeline, workloads, scaling and window
policy are therefore explicit adaptations, not an exact repro-
duction of the original method or its published accuracy.

D. Partitions, Labels and Evaluation
Executions were split 40/30/30 within each cell, with all
samples from one run kept in one partition. Payments has
32 training, 24 validation and 24 test runs; RF and SVM

contention alone is not ground truth. All runs completed and
no request errors were recorded.
The autoencoder minimizes root-mean-square reconstruc-

Adam at 10 −4, batch size 512 and at most 1000 epochs.

autoencoders reached 1000 epochs; payments/RF/SVM clas-

E. Accuracy, Cost and Workload Dependence
Table X and Fig. 4 show the complete matched comparison.
On payments, the resource-logistic model correctly classiﬁed
all 24 test runs (nine positives, ﬁfteen negatives), yielding F1
1.000; the neural adaptation missed one bursty run, yielding
0.941. Both have AUC 1.000, illustrating that validation-
selected operating thresholds matter even with identical test
ranking. On RF, all four methods classify three positives and
nine negatives correctly. On SVM, the logistic model detects
one of four positives, compared with three for the neural model
and DET-08: F1 is 0.400 versus 0.857. The lightweight method
therefore improves the payments point estimate and ties RF,
but is not consistently more accurate.
No method produced a false positive in these test sets. Nega-
tive counts are only 15/9/8 for payments/RF/SVM. A post-hoc
paired run bootstrap (5000 resamples, excluding undeﬁned F1
draws) gives 95% intervals for logistic minus neural F1 of
[0, 0.231], [0, 0], and [−1, 0], respectively. These conditional
intervals are exploratory, unadjusted for multiple comparisons
and limited by the small sample. The RF interval reﬂects
identical observed predictions, not established equivalence on
future data. The intervals do not establish a general accuracy
advantage.
Logistic ﬁtting took 0.01295/0.00629/0.00330 s
for payments/RF/SVM; the neural pipeline took
43.16/56.55/85.78 s. Batch-amortized inference took
approximately 0.00394/0.00533/0.00315 ms per run for
logistic regression, compared with 1.623/2.652/2.480 ms
for the neural classiﬁer. These timings exclude telemetry
collection and do not measure end-to-end online latency.
A production cost claim also needs repeated timing and a
monitoring-disabled comparison.
DET-08’s payments F1 of 0.500 follows from its narrower
semantics: it missed the three bursty and three disjoint-CPU
competitor positives. A 30 s sustained-sharing rule is not
designed to capture every short burst or every residual source
of degradation. The logistic extension detects those cases in
this sample, while its SVM errors show that replacing DET-08
universally would be unjustiﬁed. These observations answer
RQ5 as a workload-dependent accuracy/cost tradeoff.

IX. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
IsoLab connects container controls to reproducible security
and reliability evidence. The completed 160-run pilot demon-
strates 61.0%, 35.1% and 40.1% reductions in victim time
under sustained contention for payments, Spark RF and Spark
SVM, respectively. These gains accompany approximately 64–
66% lower competing throughput, making the allocation trade-
off explicit. The lightweight detector has a higher payments F1
point estimate and ties RF at much lower measured ﬁtting cost
than the neural adaptation, but loses accuracy on SVM. The
complete comparison supports workload-speciﬁc engineering
choices, not universal classiﬁer superiority.
Historical experiments additionally demonstrate the tested
boundary, conﬁguration and startup-integrity outcomes. To-
gether, the artifacts provide a basis for evaluating controls
and detection on common evidence without conﬂating pos-
ture scores, trigger acceptance, classiﬁer accuracy or pub-
lished literature statistics. Future work should add indepen-
dent workloads and hosts, longer observation, monitoring-
disabled measurements, individual-control ablations and robust
validation-selected operating points. Signed manifests, contin-
uous integrity observation and broader exploit-based validation
remain separate extensions.
